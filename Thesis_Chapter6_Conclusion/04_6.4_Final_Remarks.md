## 6.4 Final Remarks

This thesis asked a narrow question of a standard pipeline. Not how good a score it can be made to produce, but where inside it that score comes from. It answered by building every architecturally valid combination of four components and scoring each under one complete leave-one-subject-out protocol, so that each component could be read off matched pairs differing in nothing else. The plainest statement of what came back is that the stages do not contribute equally, that what a stage costs is a poor guide to what it returns, and that the configuration a designer would have proposed was not the configuration that scored best.

§3.10.3 puts the practical complaint directly: a practitioner assembling this pipeline today has no basis for allocating effort. This study supplies one — a matched-pair account of what each of the four stages contributes, in which one carries almost all of the measured effect, one subtracts on average while consuming most of the compute, and two cannot be separated from noise at this sample size. That is a coarser instrument than a ranking, and it is the one the evidence supports. Delivered with an explicit statement of the corpus, the protocol and the fixed preprocessing setting for which it holds, it is a more useful thing to hand a practitioner than a headline score with none.

The design is the more durable contribution. Building the matrix rather than the proposal is what made that ordering visible at all, and the experiments §6.3 sets out are ones this study is in a position to specify precisely because it was built that way.
