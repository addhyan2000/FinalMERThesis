\chapter{Results}
\label{ch:results}

This chapter reports the results of the ablation. Section~\ref{sec:results-overview} ranks the twelve configurations. Sections~\ref{sec:transformer-results} to~\ref{sec:cnn-results} report the effect of each component over its matched pairs, and Section~\ref{sec:per-class} reports per-class performance. Section~\ref{sec:literature-comparison} compares the results with published work, and Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses why the results take the form they do, including an interpretation of the confusion matrices. All scores are pooled over the 25 leave-one-subject-out folds (Section~\ref{sec:aggregation}); the primary metric is pooled macro F1, and pooled accuracy is reported alongside it. All comparisons are subject to the design limitations of Section~\ref{sec:design-limitation}: a single random seed, and checkpoint selection on the held-out subject.

\subsection{Best Configuration}
\label{sec:headline-result}

\textbf{Configuration 2 achieves the highest pooled macro F1, 0.7122.} It contains only the temporal transformer, which receives the motion features through spatial pooling and a learned projection, without the convolutional stem, SimAM or magnification. Configuration 8, which enables all four components, scores \textbf{0.6659}, a difference of \textbf{0.0463}.

Configuration 8 has a slightly higher pooled accuracy (0.7500 against 0.7436), which corresponds to 117 against 116 correctly classified clips. The two metrics therefore select different configurations. Under the primary metric, configuration 2 ranks first; the difference is a point estimate, not a statistically established advantage.

\subsection{Majority-Class Reference}
\label{sec:all-negative-reference}

A classifier that predicts Negative for every clip reaches an accuracy of $99/156=0.6346$. Its Negative-class F1 is $2\times99/(156+99)=0.7765$ and the other two class scores are zero, giving a pooled macro F1 of \textbf{0.2588}. All twelve configurations exceed this macro-F1 reference, whereas an accuracy near 0.63 is attainable without distinguishing between expressions.

Figure~\ref{fig:metric-definitions} contrasts pooled and mean-of-folds scores. Because some classes are absent from individual folds, the mean-of-folds macro F1 cannot exceed 0.6267 (Section~\ref{sec:mean-of-folds-rejected}); this ceiling does not apply to pooled macro F1.

\subsection{Separation by the Temporal Transformer}
\label{sec:transformer-split}
\label{sec:baseline-count}

The six configurations with a transformer score between 0.5830 and 0.7122, and the six without it between 0.4192 and 0.4480; the closest members of the two groups are separated by \textbf{0.1350}. No other component divides the configurations into non-overlapping groups. The baseline, configuration 4 (magnification only), scores 0.4386. Four configurations score below it (1, 3, 5 and 16), and configuration 13 scores slightly above it (0.4480) despite belonging to the group without a transformer.

\subsection{Consistency Across Pairs}
\label{sec:transformer-consistency}
\label{sec:transformer-separation}

Even the smallest transformer gain exceeds the absolute mean effect of every other component. The consistent positive direction holds across all tested architectural settings, but the six pairs share one dataset and one training protocol and are not independent replications. With a single seed and no retained per-clip predictions, neither a confidence interval nor a paired significance test can be derived from them. The separation of the ranking by the transformer is likewise a property of these twelve scores, not evidence that the same boundary would hold for another dataset, seed or training regime.

\subsection{Recognition of All Three Classes}
\label{sec:no-abandoned-class}

Every configuration achieves a nonzero pooled F1 on all three classes; the lowest value is the Surprise F1 of configuration 4, 0.2462. Every class therefore receives some correct predictions across the full evaluation, although not necessarily within every fold. This is consistent with the class-balancing rule of Section~\ref{sec:class-weighting-rule}, but the causal effect of that rule is not isolated, because the final experiment does not compare training regimes.

\subsection{Balance Across Classes}
\label{sec:config9-imbalanced}
\label{sec:config2-balanced}

Configuration 9 has the highest Negative F1 (0.8491) but the second-lowest Surprise F1 (0.3000); its difference of \textbf{0.5491} between the best and worst class is the largest in the study. Its pooled accuracy is nevertheless 0.7308, which shows that high accuracy can coincide with weak minority-class performance.

Configuration 2 has the highest Positive and Surprise F1 (0.6506 and 0.6792) and the narrowest range of class scores among the configurations with a transformer (0.1562). Compared with configuration 8, it improves Positive F1 from 0.5556 to 0.6506 and Surprise F1 from 0.5965 to 0.6792, whereas Negative F1 falls from 0.8458 to 0.8068. Its advantage in macro F1 therefore comes from the minority classes, while configuration 8's stronger majority-class performance accounts for its one-clip advantage in accuracy.

\subsection{Separation on the Negative Class}
\label{sec:negative-tracks-split}

Negative F1 separates the configurations with a transformer (0.7485 to 0.8491) from those without it (0.3500 to 0.5641), with a gap of 0.1844. For Positive and Surprise, the ranges of the two groups overlap by 0.0992 and 0.1035 respectively. The clearest single-class separation produced by the transformer therefore occurs on the largest class.

\subsection{Reliability of Minority-Class Scores}
\label{sec:minority-caution}

A single changed prediction can alter a minority-class score noticeably, although the size of the change depends on the existing counts of true positives, false positives and false negatives. Similar scores also do not imply that two configurations classified the same clips correctly. Without retained per-clip predictions or repeated seeds, small differences between configurations cannot be tested for significance or reproducibility.

\label{sec:where-lands}

\subsection{Differences in Experimental Protocol}
\label{sec:not-like-for-like}
\label{sec:comparison-supports}

Three differences prevent a direct comparison:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Training data.} The Composite Database Evaluation pools SMIC, CASME~II and SAMM, with 442 samples from 68 subjects, compared with 156 clips from 25 subjects in this study; some entries also use additional macro-expression data~\cite{see2019}. The composite dataset is approximately 2.8 times larger and covers different recording conditions, but the effect of this difference on performance cannot be quantified here.
	\item \textbf{Evaluation data.} The CASME~II subset of MEGC contains 145 clips, whereas this study uses 156, because sadness and fear are included in Negative (Section~\ref{sec:label-taxonomy}). The evaluated samples are therefore not identical.
	\item \textbf{Checkpoint selection.} This study selects the epoch on the held-out subject that is later scored (Section~\ref{sec:checkpoint-selection}), which may bias its scores upwards by an unknown amount. This bias cannot explain the gap, nor can its size be estimated.
\end{itemize}

The comparison therefore locates the results of this study within the range of published results while making the protocol differences explicit. It does not establish state-of-the-art performance or superiority over the two lower entries. The principal evidence of this thesis is the comparison of components within one pipeline under one protocol.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
\label{sec:summary-limitations}
% =============================================================================

This section summarises the component effects and discusses why the results take the form they do. The explanations below are grounded in the architecture and in the confusion matrices, but none was isolated experimentally; they are the most plausible accounts of the observed results, not demonstrated causes.

\subsection{Summary of Component Effects}
\label{sec:four-findings}
\label{sec:headline-tension}

Table~\ref{tab:component-effects} collects the mean effect of each component, and Figure~\ref{fig:component-effects} shows all twenty matched differences. The transformer has the largest effect and improves every pair; magnification has a small and mixed effect, including a loss for the best configuration; SimAM has a mean close to zero that conceals trade-offs between classes; and the convolutional stem has a negative mean driven by one large loss. Consequently, the configuration with all four components is not the best: enabling every component does not maximise pooled macro F1, and the effects are not additive.

		Motion magnification      & +0.0152            & 6 & 4 / 6 \\

\subsection{Interpretation of the Confusion Matrices}
\label{sec:confusion-discussion}

Figure~\ref{fig:confusion-matrices} shows the pooled confusion matrices of three configurations. Each row corresponds to a true class and each column to a predicted class, so the diagonal contains the correctly classified clips and each off-diagonal cell counts one type of error. Figure~\ref{fig:prediction-distribution} complements them by showing, for every configuration, how often each class is predicted compared with how often it occurs.

	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig5_6_confusion_matrices.png}
	\caption[Pooled confusion matrices for three configurations]{Pooled confusion matrices, summed over all 25 folds, for \texttt{config\_1} (no components), \texttt{config\_8} (all four components) and \texttt{config\_2} (the best configuration). Rows are true classes and columns predicted classes; each cell gives the clip count and the row-normalised recall.}
	\label{fig:confusion-matrices}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig5_10_prediction_distribution.png}
	\caption[Predicted class counts against true class sizes]{Number of clips predicted as each class by every configuration (bars), against the true number of clips in each class (dashed lines). Configurations are ordered as in Table~\ref{tab:twelve-ranked}.}
	\label{fig:prediction-distribution}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Configurations without the transformer over-predict the minority classes.} The six configurations without a transformer predict Surprise between 40 and 97 times, although only 25 clips are Surprise, and all six predict Positive more often than its 32 clips (38 to 59 times). As a result, most Negative clips are misclassified: their Negative recall is only 0.21 to 0.44, although their Negative precision is high (0.77 to 1.00). In configuration 1, for example, 35 of the 99 Negative clips are predicted as Surprise and 22 as Positive (Figure~\ref{fig:confusion-matrices}, left). This explains why their accuracy (0.40 to 0.48) is lower than that of the all-Negative classifier, although their macro F1 is higher. A likely cause is the interaction between class-balanced sampling and weak features. The sampler presents the three classes equally often during training (Section~\ref{sec:balanced-sampling}), so the model learns class frequencies close to one third each rather than the true 99 : 32 : 25 distribution~\cite{he2009}. Buda et al. accordingly recommend that, after oversampling, the decision threshold be adjusted to the true class priors when the overall number of correct classifications matters~\cite{buda2018}, a step not taken here. When the input features discriminate poorly between classes, predictions follow this learned prior, and the minority classes are predicted far more often than they occur.

\textbf{Configurations with the transformer predict the class distribution more faithfully.} Their Negative recall rises to between 0.65 and 0.91, and their predicted class counts lie much closer to the true counts (Figure~\ref{fig:prediction-distribution}). The best configuration, configuration 2, recognises all three classes with similar recall (0.72 for Negative, 0.84 for Positive and 0.72 for Surprise). Its dominant error is to predict Positive for Negative clips (20 of 99), which lowers its Positive precision to 0.53.

\textbf{The convolutional stem shifts the predictions of the transformer configurations towards the majority class.} The four configurations with both the stem and the transformer predict Negative 82 to 113 times and have a Positive recall of only 0.28 to 0.63, whereas the two configurations with the transformer but without the stem predict Negative 72 and 77 times and reach a Positive recall of 0.81 and 0.84. In configuration 8, 13 of the 32 Positive clips are predicted as Negative (Figure~\ref{fig:confusion-matrices}, centre), and in configuration 9, 12 of the 25 Surprise clips are. This shift raises Negative recall and accuracy but lowers the minority-class scores, which explains why configuration 8 has the highest accuracy but not the highest macro F1.

\textbf{Most confusions involve the Negative class.} In the transformer configurations, confusions between Negative and the other two classes are considerably more frequent than confusions between Positive and Surprise. This is consistent with Negative being the largest and most heterogeneous class, since it combines disgust, repression, sadness and fear (Section~\ref{sec:three-class-grouping}), and with the observation of Yan et al. that a single action unit can indicate several emotions, for example AU4 (frown) disgust, anger, attention or tension, so that the CASME~II labels are only partly based on action units~\cite{yan2014}. Because per-clip predictions were not retained, it cannot be determined which of the four original emotions account for these confusions.

\subsection{Effect of the Temporal Transformer}
\label{sec:transformer-interpretation}

The large effect of the transformer is best understood from what its replacement discards. Without the transformer, the sequence of 32 frame features is averaged over time. In the configurations without the stem, the replacement projection is linear, so averaging after it is equivalent to classifying the time-averaged, spatially pooled motion field. Averaging the flow between consecutive frames over a clip approximates the net displacement between onset and offset. Because a micro-expression rises to its apex and then returns towards neutral (Figure~\ref{fig:casme2-sequence})~\cite{yan2014}, the displacements of the rising and falling phases partly cancel, and the order in which they occur is lost entirely. The transformer receives the same features, but its positional encoding and self-attention~\cite{vaswani2017} allow it to relate the onset, apex and offset phases, which is the temporal structure that sequence-based methods aim to exploit~\cite{zhang2022,xia2020a}. The transformer also adds approximately 349,000 parameters, so the effect of temporal modelling cannot be separated from the effect of added capacity.

Transformers can perform poorly when trained on insufficient data~\cite{dosovitskiy2021}, yet the transformer is effective here with 156 clips. A plausible reason is that it operates on a short sequence of 32 low-dimensional tokens derived from analytically computed motion features, rather than on raw images, which reduces what it must learn from the data.

\subsection{Effect of the Convolutional Stem}
\label{sec:cnn-interpretation-result}

Three architectural factors offer plausible explanations for the weak and sometimes negative effect of the stem. First, the stem's output is reduced to a single average per channel over the whole frame before it reaches the transformer, which discards where in the face a movement occurs. The replacement operation instead keeps a coarse $4\times4$ grid of the motion field. Because micro-expressions are local movements~\cite{ben2021}, their location may be more informative than the learned features that the stem provides; this is consistent with the largest loss occurring when the stem is added to configuration 2. Second, the input already consists of optical flow and strain, which make motion and deformation explicit, so there is little left for a shallow spatial extractor to add~\cite{liong2019b,see2019}. Third, the stem learns spatial filters at $224\times224$ on uncropped frames from only 156 clips, whereas the reviewed evidence favours shallow models and lower resolutions for this amount of data~\cite{xia2020b,li2022survey}. The stem also changes the model's capacity, and kernel shape, resolution and placement were not varied, so these explanations cannot be separated.

\subsection{Effect of Magnification}
\label{sec:evm-interpretation-result}

Magnification has a small and inconsistent effect, in contrast to the clear gains reported for appearance-based descriptors~\cite{liX2018,bai2021}. Several factors plausibly contribute. Optical flow already recovers faint motion, and per-channel normalisation removes differences in overall amplitude, so the representation gains less from amplification than an intensity-based descriptor. Without face alignment, magnification also amplifies head movement and noise within the pass-band~\cite{liX2018}. Because magnification is applied after sampling, the realised pass-band depends on the clip length (Section~\ref{sec:three-channel-tensor}). Finally, the reported optimum of the amplification factor was found for appearance-based descriptors~\cite{liX2018}, and $\alpha = 10$ was not tuned for a motion representation.

\subsection{Effect of Parameter-Free Attention}
\label{sec:simam-discussion}

The negligible mean effect of SimAM follows largely from its formulation. Because the reciprocal energy in \eqref{eq:simam-gate} is at least $1/2$, the sigmoid weight lies between approximately 0.62 and 1, so SimAM can only attenuate less distinctive activations moderately~\cite{yang2021}. Its output is then averaged over the whole frame, which further dilutes a re-weighting of individual positions. The statistics are also computed over the entire clip rather than per frame, and the regularisation constant was not tuned for this task. The module can nevertheless shift the balance between classes, as the trade-off in the $9\rightarrow6$ pair shows.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:ch5-limitations}

The limitations of Section~\ref{sec:gaps-limitations} apply to all results in this chapter. The following are most important for their interpretation:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Single seed.} Each configuration is evaluated once, so no confidence intervals are available for the differences between configurations.
	\item \textbf{Checkpoint selection on the evaluation fold.} Epoch selection and final scoring use the same held-out subject, and the resulting bias cannot be estimated.
	\item \textbf{No per-clip predictions.} The pooled confusion matrices cannot show which clips two configurations classify differently, which prevents paired significance tests.
	\item \textbf{Limited data.} The 156 clips come from 25 subjects of one dataset with a narrow demographic range and laboratory recording, so generalisation beyond these conditions is not measured.
	\item \textbf{Fixed training settings.} No hyperparameter search was performed, so a component could behave differently under other settings.
	\item \textbf{Presence or absence only.} Each component is varied only as present or absent; resolution, kernel shape, magnification settings and preprocessing order are fixed, and the replacement operations also change model capacity.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:ch5-conclusion}
% =============================================================================

This chapter reported the results of the ablation. The temporal transformer had by far the largest effect, improving pooled macro F1 by 0.2173 on average and in all six matched pairs. Magnification had a small and inconsistent effect, SimAM a negligible one, and the convolutional stem a negative mean effect. The best configuration, with the transformer alone, reached a pooled macro F1 of 0.7122 and outperformed the configuration with all four components. The confusion matrices showed that configurations without the transformer over-predict the minority classes, whereas the stem shifts transformer configurations towards the majority class. The results lie within the range of published results on CASME~II, although differences in protocol prevent a direct comparison.

Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion} draws the conclusions of the thesis: it states its contributions, the limits within which they hold and the work that remains.
