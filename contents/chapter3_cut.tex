\chapter{Literature Review}
\label{ch:literature}

This chapter reviews the published evidence for each component of the recognition pipeline evaluated in this thesis. Section~\ref{sec:evm-review} reviews Eulerian video magnification, \autoref{sec:flow-review} optical flow, \autoref{sec:strain-review} optical strain and \autoref{sec:tim-review} temporal normalisation. Section~\ref{sec:cnn-review}, \autoref{sec:simam-review} and \autoref{sec:transformer-review} review the learned components: the convolutional backbone, parameter-free attention and the temporal transformer. Section~\ref{sec:imbalance-review} reviews training under class imbalance. For each component, the review states what the published studies found and what their evidence does not establish. Section~\ref{sec:synthesis-and-gap} combines these findings into the research gap and lists every point at which the implemented system departs from the reviewed methods.

\section{Motion Magnification: Eulerian Video Magnification}
\label{sec:evm-review}
% =============================================================================

Eulerian video magnification was introduced by Wu et al.~\cite{wu2012} and has been applied to micro-expression recognition by Bai et al.~\cite{bai2021}, Li et al.~\cite{liX2018}, and Li, Huang and Zhao~\cite{liY2018,liY2021}. Its mechanism is described in \autoref{sec:motion-magnification}.

High-speed recording~\cite{yan2014} (\autoref{sec:why-spontaneous}) addresses the short duration of micro-expressions but not their low intensity. A movement spread across dozens of frames at 200~fps may still change only a few grey levels over a small area of skin, and Yan et al.~\cite{yan2014} report discarding samples because the movement was too faint to place an onset. Li et al.~\cite{liX2018} propose amplifying the motion before any descriptor is computed, so that the differences between categories are enlarged rather than the classifier being made more sensitive. Because magnification operates entirely before the model, it is a clean factor for ablation.

Three parameters must be chosen: the temporal pass-band, which determines \textit{which} motions are amplified; the amplification factor $\alpha$, which determines \textit{by how much}; and the pyramid depth~\cite{wu2012}. For the pass-band, Bai et al.~\cite{bai2021} report the rule of the original authors that the band should match the duration of the phenomenon, which for micro-expressions is 1/25 to 1/5~s, or \textbf{5--25 Hz}. They found that this full band produced ``noticeable noise'' in their setting and narrowed it to 15--25 Hz~\cite{bai2021}. This is the only explicit guidance on band selection in the reviewed literature.

\subsection{Evidence that Magnification Improves Recognition}
\label{sec:evm-evidence}

The strongest evidence comes from Li et al.~\cite{liX2018}, who sweep the magnification factor over $\alpha \in \{1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24, 30\}$, with $\alpha = 1$ denoting no magnification, on SMIC and CASME~II under leave-one-subject-out evaluation. Two findings emerge.

First, \textbf{magnification helps consistently}, ``for all three kinds of features on all four testing datasets''~\cite{liX2018}, and substantially on CASME~II (\autoref{tab:evm-effect}). Their best configuration reaches 67.21\,\% on CASME~II~\cite{liX2018}, which was competitive with the state of the art at the time.

Second, \textbf{the relationship between $\alpha$ and accuracy is non-monotonic}. Li et al.~\cite{liX2018} describe the curves as ``rainbow-shaped'', with the best performance ``generally achieved when the motion is magnified in the range of $[8, 16]$''. They explain both failure modes~\cite{liX2018}: too little magnification ``might not be enough to reveal the ME motion progress'', whereas too much ``degrades the performance because too many artifacts are induced''. Magnification is therefore a parameter with an interior optimum rather than an improvement to be maximised.

Bai et al.~\cite{bai2021} provide a second demonstration with a different downstream model, a pre-trained VGGFace2 encoder followed by a bidirectional LSTM. On SMIC they report \textbf{75.76\,\% with phase-based magnification against a 60.60\,\% baseline}, with phase-based magnification outperforming the amplitude-based variant~\cite{bai2021}. The baseline, however, is taken from an earlier publication rather than from a condition without magnification in their own experiment, so it is not a matched control~\cite{bai2021}. Taken together, the two studies indicate that the benefit is not specific to one feature family, at least when the downstream representation is based on appearance.

Li, Huang and Zhao~\cite{liY2018,liY2021} apply magnification in a different setting: they magnify a single detected apex frame with \textbf{$\alpha = 30$}, at the upper end of the sweep of Li et al., and augment the data with the two frames on either side of the apex. This value is stated rather than derived, and the two settings differ substantially (a short window treated as static appearance, against full magnified sequences described by spatio-temporal descriptors), so the two values do not contradict each other. The safer conclusion is that $\alpha$ depends on the \textit{combination} of magnification and downstream representation and cannot be transferred between pipelines without re-validation.

\subsection{Known Limitations}
\label{sec:evm-limitations}

The reviewed studies document four limitations.

\textbf{Magnification amplifies all motion within the band, not only expressions.} Li et al.~\cite{liX2018} do not use magnification for micro-expression \textit{spotting} ``because it magnifies unwanted motions (e.g. head movements) at the same time''. A rigid head movement whose temporal frequency falls within the pass-band is amplified in the same way as a brow raise~\cite{liX2018}. Alignment and cropping reduce this effect but do not eliminate it.

\textbf{Artefacts increase with $\alpha$}, as all four papers report~\cite{bai2021,liX2018,liY2018,liY2021}. \textbf{No principled procedure exists for choosing $\alpha$}: Li et al.~\cite{liX2018} obtain their value by exhaustive search, whereas the apex-frame studies state theirs~\cite{liY2018,liY2021}. Finally, \textbf{every evaluation uses an appearance-based downstream representation}: LBP, HOG and HIGO in Li et al.~\cite{liX2018}, VGGFace2 features in Bai et al.~\cite{bai2021} and VGG-Face in both studies of Li, Huang and Zhao. Each is computed on magnified pixel intensities. \textbf{No reviewed study measures the contribution of magnification when the classifier receives an explicit motion field.}

\section{Motion Representation: Dense Optical Flow}
\label{sec:flow-review}
% =============================================================================

Dense optical flow~\cite{horn1981} supplies the horizontal and vertical displacement channels of the input. The relevant literature concerns three choices: the estimator, the frame pairs to which it is applied, and whether flow serves as a recognition feature or as an alignment tool. The estimation principle is described in \autoref{sec:optical-flow}.

Xu et al.~\cite{xu2017} motivate motion descriptors by the difficulty of interpreting micro-expressions through texture alone: flow describes apparent movement directly, whereas image intensity also records appearance and illumination. This does not make flow independent of identity or recording conditions, since facial geometry, alignment and brightness changes still affect the estimate. The benchmark evidence supports the choice: the three highest-ranked MEGC 2019 submissions used optical flow~\cite{see2019}, OFF-ApexNet~\cite{liong2019a} uses horizontal and vertical flow as network inputs, STSTNet~\cite{liong2019b} adds strain, and Zhao et al.~\cite{zhaoS2021} use a sequence of flow fields. This convergence is, however, not a controlled demonstration that motion always outperforms appearance.

\textbf{The choice of estimator is not settled.} Zhao et al.~\cite{zhaoS2021} use the Farneb\"ack method~\cite{farneback2003} through OpenCV, citing its availability; OFF-ApexNet~\cite{liong2019a} uses TV-L1, citing its preservation of motion discontinuities; and Xu et al.~\cite{xu2017} use the method of Sun, Roth and Black. STSTNet~\cite{liong2019b} computes onset-to-apex flow without naming its estimator, so TV-L1 cannot be attributed to it on the basis of its similarity to OFF-ApexNet. The advantages claimed for TV-L1 have not been measured as a recognition gain over Farneb\"ack on CASME~II, since no controlled comparison of estimators exists. This thesis uses Farneb\"ack and leaves the comparison open.

\textbf{The choice between onset-to-apex flow and a flow sequence is consequential.} OFF-ApexNet~\cite{liong2019a} and STSTNet~\cite{liong2019b} represent each clip by the flow from onset to apex, resized to $28\times28$. This is an economical summary of the displacement from the neutral to the peak expression, suited to a small training set, but it omits the intermediate trajectory and the relaxation after the apex. Zhao et al.~\cite{zhaoS2021} instead select eleven key frames and compute ten flow fields between adjacent frames, retaining the change of motion across the expression. The distinction is between a single displacement summary and a sequence whose order a temporal model can exploit. This thesis follows the sequence approach but samples existing frames uniformly rather than selecting key frames, which provides a temporal input for the transformer ablation. It does not measure the effect of replacing the sequence with a single onset-to-apex pair.

Flow is also used for alignment. Xu et al.~\cite{xu2017} align faces coarsely by landmarks and then refine the alignment within the sequence using pixel-level motion, because, as they note, a small misalignment can considerably degrade the recognition of such subtle movements. This pipeline performs no flow-based alignment, and this refinement remains the most promising unused improvement to its preprocessing.

\textbf{Limitations of the representation.} Flow remains sensitive to violations of brightness constancy~\cite{horn1981}, to alignment error and to estimator settings~\cite{xu2017}. Apex-based systems additionally depend on selecting an informative frame, and OFF-ApexNet~\cite{liong2019a} attributes its weaker SMIC performance to imprecise apex selection. An onset-to-offset sequence avoids dependence on one selected frame but still relies on the clip boundaries and the sampling: repeated frames yield near-zero flow, and large sampling intervals make the small-motion approximation less appropriate~\cite{horn1981}.

\section{Deformation Representation: Optical Strain}
\label{sec:strain-review}
% =============================================================================

Optical strain supplies the third input channel. Its definition is given in \autoref{sec:optical-strain}; this section reviews the published evidence and one consequential divergence.

Flow describes displacement, whereas strain describes its local spatial variation~\cite{shreve2011,liong2016}. A spatially uniform translation has zero displacement gradient, whereas neighbouring regions of skin moving differently produce deformation~\cite{shreve2011}. Shreve et al.~\cite{shreve2011} use this distinction to detect facial activity and describe robustness to moderate head translation, which does not imply robustness to arbitrary head movement or registration error. Their statements about robustness to illumination and make-up~\cite{shreve2011} are attributed to earlier studies rather than measured, and are treated here as motivation rather than evidence.

\subsection{The Implemented Magnitude and its Divergence}
\label{sec:strain-divergence}

Liong et al.~\cite{liong2014a,liong2014b,liong2016} reduce the strain tensor to a scalar using the Frobenius norm of all four components, which counts the two equal shear entries separately. \textbf{The implementation in this thesis counts shear once}, giving the three-term magnitude of \eqref{eq:strain-magnitude}. Spatial derivatives are computed by central differences at one-pixel spacing, following Liong et al.~\cite{liong2014a}, but the scalar reduction differs from their convention.

STSTNet~\cite{liong2019b} cannot serve as a precedent for the three-term form. Its printed magnitude equation~\cite{liong2019b} repeats $\partial u$ in the mixed derivative where the symmetric tensor requires $\partial v/\partial x$ alongside $\partial u/\partial y$, and it places the factor one-half \emph{outside} the squared sum. Correcting the repeated derivative while keeping this placement yields the \textit{four}-term convention; moving the factor inside the square would yield the implemented form, but that requires a further change to the printed expression. Because the intended form cannot be determined from the published equation, this thesis declares a departure from the four-term convention rather than claiming to reproduce STSTNet.

The four-term form weights pure shear by a factor of $\sqrt{2}$ relative to the implemented form. Min--max normalisation removes a uniform scale factor but not this difference, because normal and shear contributions vary across pixels and time. The two conventions were not compared experimentally, so their practical equivalence is not assumed.

\subsection{Recognition Evidence and its Protocol Limits}
\label{sec:strain-evidence}

Strain has been used in three ways~\cite{liong2014a,liong2014b,liong2016,liong2019b}: as a pooled descriptor (optical strain features, OSF), as a spatial weighting of LBP-TOP histograms (optical strain weighted features, OSW), and as a direct input channel. Liong et al.~\cite{liong2016} combine the first two and report the results in \autoref{tab:strain-performance}; their earlier work also applies Wiener and Gaussian filters to suppress noise~\cite{liong2014a}. STSTNet~\cite{liong2019b} uses strain directly as an input channel alongside onset-to-apex flow, which is the precedent for the three-channel input used here, although this thesis retains all three channels across successive frame pairs rather than reducing each clip to a single map.

On CASME~II, the evidence depends on how strain is used and against what. OSF alone performs below the texture baseline, whereas the combined descriptor improves on it by 0.41 to 1.22 points across block settings~\cite{liong2016}. Liong et al.~\cite{liong2016} also test flow features and weights within the same framework, reaching 55.87\,\% against 63.16\,\% for the strain pair. This is a \textbf{matched comparison of flow and strain}, so the literature does isolate strain against flow. The comparison, however, concerns hand-crafted descriptors under a protocol that is not subject-independent, and its accuracy gains are not comparable with pooled macro F1. It does not establish the gain from adding a strain channel to a learned sequence model.

\section{Temporal Normalisation: Fixed-Length Sampling}
\label{sec:tim-review}
% =============================================================================

Flow and strain determine \textit{what} is computed from each frame pair; temporal normalisation determines \textit{which} pairs, and how many. The Temporal Interpolation Model (TIM) is attributed to Zhou et al. by Li et al.~\cite{liX2013,liX2018} and to Pfister et al.~\cite{pfister2011} by Lu et al.~\cite{lu2015}.

Normalisation is necessary because a batched network requires inputs of fixed shape, whereas clip lengths in CASME~II vary by a factor of four (\autoref{sec:corpus-threats}). Duration is also a nuisance variable: Lu et al.~\cite{lu2015} normalise length to remove ``temporal fluctuations of micro-expressions, which may introduce noise irrelevant to classification''. The same authors~\cite{lu2015} describe TIM as ``a manifold-based interpolation method, which builds a low-dimensional embedding of an image sequence, and then interpolates a curve in the low-dimensional space. The interpolated frames are mapped back to the original high-dimensional space.'' TIM therefore \textbf{synthesises frames that were never recorded}; it was proposed ``to generate sufficient frames from an image sequence'' and is an upsampler as well as a length normaliser.

\textbf{Ten frames is a convention rather than a finding, and the studies that test it disagree.} TIM appears in the literature as a routine preprocessing step, almost always with ten frames~\cite{liX2013,liX2018,lu2015}. Li et al.~\cite{liX2018} vary the interpolation length from 10 to 80 frames on SMIC with the descriptor held fixed and report that ``the best performance for all three datasets is achieved with interpolation to 10 frames'', because ``in longer sequences, changes along the time-dimension are diluted''. Ben et al.~\cite{ben2021} reach the opposite conclusion: with alignment, descriptor and classifier held fixed, they interpolate to between 10 and 110 frames using both Newton interpolation and TIM on MMEW, finding the best results at 60 frames for Newton interpolation (33.3\,\%) and at 30 or 60 frames for TIM (\textbf{38.9\,\%}), and adopt TIM with 60 frames. In both studies, accuracy is non-monotonic in length. Xu et al.~\cite{xu2017} report the only sweep across several datasets (10, 15 and 20 frames on CASME, CASME~II and both SMIC subsets) and use \textbf{linear} interpolation ``instead of Temporal Interpolation Model because in a high frame-rate scenario, linear interpolation suffices to characterize the motion pattern with less error''.

Two caveats limit what these results imply for this thesis. The experiment of Ben et al.~\cite{ben2021} uses MMEW with LBP-TOP, not CASME~II or a learned model, and operates mainly by \textit{upsampling}, so its optimum need not hold when frames are discarded. More fundamentally, \textbf{frame synthesis conflicts with motion analysis}: synthesised frames are harmless for an appearance-based representation, but for a motion representation they produce synthesised displacements. Xu et al.~\cite{xu2017} come closest to this point, preferring linear interpolation to TIM because it characterises the motion pattern with less error at high frame rates, but they do not compare the two. No reviewed study compares interpolation methods with a motion representation.

\section{The Learned Spatial Backbone: Shallow Convolutional Networks}
\label{sec:cnn-review}
% =============================================================================

By 2019 the highest-ranked challenge submissions all used optical flow as their input~\cite{see2019}; the open question was which model should process it. A learned backbone offers what hand-crafted descriptors cannot~\cite{liong2019b}, but it is constrained by the small size of the available datasets~\cite{li2022survey} (\autoref{sec:corpus-threats}).

\textbf{The term ``3D CNN''~\cite{ji2013,tran2015} denotes three different architectures in this literature.} STSTNet~\cite{liong2019b}, the reference shallow model, describes itself as a ``Shallow Triple Stream Three-dimensional CNN'', but its input is a single $28 \times 28 \times 3$ tensor whose third dimension holds horizontal flow, vertical flow and strain, \textbf{not time}. Its three parallel single-layer streams~\cite{liong2019b}, with 3, 5 and 8 kernels, compensate for ``the small scale input data \ldots to avoid the problem of underfitting''; the clip has already been reduced to one frame pair. STRCN~\cite{xia2020a}, by contrast, extends convolutional connectivity ``across temporal domain'' with recurrent convolutional layers, and Zhao et al.~\cite{zhaoS2021} apply a C3D-style network~\cite{tran2015} to a ten-frame flow sequence, in which the temporal axis is present but short. Results reported under the same label are therefore not directly comparable.

\textbf{The central controlled finding is that reducing both model depth and input size helps.} Xia et al.~\cite{xia2020b} treat depth and input resolution as controlled variables, building four models ranging from a single convolutional layer to one convolutional layer followed by three recurrent convolutional layers, and evaluating each at input resolutions from 20 to 300 pixels on optical flow. Deeper models perform worse: models 3 and 4 ``have worse recognition performances compared to shallower \ldots models'', although all four are far shallower than ResNet-101~\cite{he2016}. Higher resolution also performs worse beyond a threshold~\cite{xia2020b}: performance ``will be degraded with larger input resolutions'' and, for the deeper models, ``begins to decrease dramatically when the resolution is bigger than \textbf{100 $\times$ 100}'', whereas shallow models are ``basically robust to the change of input resolution''. Both this study and STSTNet were developed for the MEGC composite setting~\cite{xia2020b,liong2019b,see2019}, which provides considerably more training data per fold than a single dataset (\autoref{sec:megc-metrics}), so their conclusions about affordable model capacity understate the constraint here. Xia et al.~\cite{xia2020b} also control capacity in a third way, which this thesis follows, by developing ``three parameter-free modules \ldots integrate with RCN without increasing any learnable parameters'', so that representational power grows without increasing the risk of overfitting.

\textbf{No reviewed study isolates the backbone itself.} The literature contains matched comparisons: it compares descriptor-based and learned methods on the same folds~\cite{liong2019b,see2019}, and Xia et al.~\cite{xia2020b} vary individual modules against a common baseline with all other parameters held constant. These comparisons, however, all \textit{add} components to a backbone that remains in place. None removes the learned spatial stem and passes the motion representation directly to a classifier or temporal model, so the contribution of the stem is not measured.

\section{Parameter-Free Attention: SimAM}
\label{sec:simam-review}
% =============================================================================

SimAM re-weights convolutional feature maps without adding learned parameters. Yang et al.~\cite{yang2021} provide the mechanism and its evidence on image recognition, and Xia et al.~\cite{xia2020b} motivate parameter-free modules for micro-expression recognition on the composite dataset, which supports the design principle but does not validate SimAM for this task. The mechanism is described in \autoref{sec:simam}.

Yang et al.~\cite{yang2021} motivate the module by spatial suppression in neuroscience: neurons that are distinguishable from their surroundings are assigned greater importance, expressed as the linear separability of one activation from the others in its channel. This is an assumption about activation statistics, not a detector of action units or minority classes; a distinctive response may reflect useful deformation, noise or an artefact. Its suitability for features derived from optical flow must therefore be evaluated. Two details of the original formulation are relevant. The shared variance printed with equation~(5) of the paper~\cite{yang2021} divides by $M$, whereas the reference code in its Figure~3 divides by $M-1$; this thesis follows the code. And the ``three-dimensional weights'' of the module refer to $C \times H \times W$ with $M = HW$ per frame, not to a temporal volume, so extending the statistics across frames is an additional design decision~\cite{yang2021}.

Yang et al.~\cite{yang2021} evaluate SimAM with several network families on CIFAR and ImageNet and report performance competitive with learned attention modules. For CIFAR they sweep $\lambda$~\cite{yang2021} from $10^{-1}$ to $10^{-6}$ and select $10^{-4}$, whereas for ImageNet they select $0.1$. The value $10^{-4}$ is therefore selected for a particular dataset rather than being a general setting.

\textbf{The transfer of these results is limited.} The original experiments~\cite{yang2021} concern natural images, different training-set sizes and different feature distributions. Here the input is flow and strain, so distinctiveness need not relate to useful evidence in the same way, and the absence of learned weights does not remove the risk of overfitting.

\subsection{Relevance and Scope}
\label{sec:simam-gap}

This thesis evaluates SimAM with motion input on a small dataset through four matched comparisons under complete leave-one-subject-out evaluation. It tests the implemented module as a whole rather than each design choice. Three questions remain open: whether the distinctiveness assumption suits flow-derived features, the effect of re-tuning $\lambda$, and whether per-frame or per-clip statistics perform better.

\section{The Temporal Encoder: Transformers}
\label{sec:transformer-review}
% =============================================================================

The temporal encoder is motivated by the Short and Long Range Relation Based Spatio-Temporal Transformer (SLSTT) of Zhang et al.~\cite{zhang2022} and by the Vision Transformer (ViT)~\cite{dosovitskiy2021} on which it builds, both based on the transformer architecture of Vaswani et al.~\cite{vaswani2017}. The component evaluated here differs substantially from SLSTT, particularly in the axis over which self-attention operates, and this section distinguishes the two.

A micro-expression evolves through onset, apex and relaxation~\cite{yan2014}, so retaining a motion sequence allows a model to relate separated stages rather than compressing them immediately into one descriptor. Zhang et al.~\cite{zhang2022} motivate their design by the need to capture both local and global patterns. This thesis compares a temporal transformer with mean pooling over the same sequence: mean pooling discards order, whereas positional encoding and self-attention allow relations between frame-pair features to be modelled~\cite{vaswani2017}. ViT~\cite{dosovitskiy2021} divides an image into patches, projects them into tokens, adds learned positional embeddings and relates the tokens through attention. Dosovitskiy et al.~\cite{dosovitskiy2021} also show that, lacking the locality and translation-equivariance of convolution, image transformers can perform poorly when trained on insufficient data, whereas large-scale pre-training improves them. This is a caution for a study with 156 clips, although it does not imply that every transformer must be pre-trained.

\textbf{Published SLSTT combines three stages}~\cite{zhang2022}. First, \textbf{onset-referenced optical flow} describes each sampled frame relative to the onset rather than to its predecessor, which Zhang et al.~\cite{zhang2022} argue gives a more structured trajectory towards and away from the apex; they use it throughout, without an ablation against short-term flow. Second, a \textbf{spatial ViT encoder} processes each flow field as patches, using an ImageNet-pre-trained ViT-B/16~\cite{dosovitskiy2021} with twelve layers at $384 \times 384$ pixels. Third, an \textbf{LSTM} combines the per-frame features, with a mean aggregator as the comparison variant. Self-attention in this architecture is spatial, and recurrence models time.

\subsection{The Aggregation Comparison}
\label{sec:slstt-results}

The comparison relevant here is internal to SLSTT and keeps the spatial transformer in both variants: on CASME~II, UF1 rises from \textbf{0.844} with the mean aggregator to \textbf{0.901} with the LSTM, a difference of 0.057~\cite{zhang2022}. This supports comparing aggregation methods within that framework. It is \textbf{not} an estimate of what an LSTM would add in this thesis, where mean pooling follows \emph{temporal} self-attention rather than independent spatial encoding, and it isolates the aggregator rather than the transformer, since both variants contain one.

The protocol labels require care. Zhang et al.~\cite{zhang2022} describe three-class leave-one-subject-out evaluations on the datasets separately and together under a Composite Database Evaluation heading, so the heading alone does not establish that every CASME~II result uses pooled cross-dataset training. They also report a Sole Database Evaluation with the original labels, including five CASME~II classes~\cite{zhang2022}. Neither setting is equivalent to the 156-clip three-class set of this thesis. SLSTT also uses a pre-trained spatial encoder, so its results do not isolate the benefit of a transformer trained from scratch on this motion representation.

\subsection{Implementation and Declared Divergences}
\label{sec:transformer-implications}

The temporal encoder evaluated in this thesis differs from published SLSTT in six respects, listed in \autoref{tab:slstt-divergences}.

\end{table}

Six matched pairs evaluate the encoder against direct mean pooling, with all other components fixed within each pair. This thesis therefore establishes a matched comparison of the implemented encoder against an order-blind baseline within this pipeline; it neither validates SLSTT nor ranks attention, recurrence and convolution in general.

\section{Training Under Severe Class Imbalance}
\label{sec:imbalance-review}
% =============================================================================

The 99 : 32 : 25 class distribution of \autoref{sec:label-taxonomy} reflects what can be elicited in a laboratory~\cite{yan2014} rather than a sampling accident, so it cannot be corrected by collecting more data with the same paradigm. The literature corrects for imbalance at three points (the data, the loss and the metric)~\cite{he2009,zhaoS2021,see2019}, and this thesis uses all three. The mechanisms are described in \autoref{sec:focal-loss} and \autoref{sec:balanced-sampling}.

\textbf{At the loss.} Focal loss was developed for object detection~\cite{lin2017}. Zhao et al.~\cite{zhaoS2021} introduce it into micro-expression recognition ``to alleviate the inefficient model training caused by the class imbalance in the MEs Datasets''. In their formulation~\cite{zhaoS2021}, the two parameters play different roles: $\gamma$ re-weights by \textit{difficulty} and is treated as a constant, ``set as 2 in practice'', whereas $\alpha$ re-weights by \textit{class frequency} and is ``treated as a hyper-parameter to set by cross validation''. Zhao et al.~\cite{zhaoS2021} compare focal loss with cross-entropy over a range of $\alpha$ values but keep $\gamma$ fixed, so the focusing parameter has not been validated for this task.

\textbf{At the data.} Xia et al.~\cite{xia2020a} combine ``temporal data augmentation strategies as well as a balanced loss \ldots jointly'' to address ``limited and imbalanced training samples''. Their ablation~\cite{xia2020a} removes each correction in turn and finds that the balanced loss improves performance only ``slightly'', by at most about two points and in one case at a cost in accuracy. This is the only controlled study of combining two corrections in the reviewed literature, and no reviewed paper reports the combination failing.

\textbf{At the metric.} MEGC 2019 requires macro-averaged metrics pooled across folds~\cite{see2019} (\autoref{sec:pooled-vs-fold}). Liong et al.~\cite{liong2016} add an argument from the subject level, reporting F1, precision and recall because these ``provide a more meaningful perspective than accuracy rates when the datasets used are naturally imbalanced since each subject has a different number of video samples.'' Both arguments apply here, where the classes are imbalanced \textbf{and} subjects contribute between 1 and 33 clips.

\textbf{Label smoothing}~\cite{szegedy2016} \textbf{has not been examined for this task.} Dosovitskiy et al.~\cite{dosovitskiy2021} list it among the regularisers they tune, but no reviewed micro-expression study uses it or reports its effect under class imbalance.

\subsection{Relevance and Scope}
\label{sec:imbalance-gap}

The corrections themselves are well established. The literature does not settle how two corrections interact, what value $\gamma$ should take for this task, or how over-correction manifests when it occurs. This thesis holds the imbalance treatment, $\gamma = 2$ and label smoothing of 0.05 constant across all twelve configurations, so it does not measure their effects. The class-collapse failures observed during its development are reported as context rather than as a controlled finding (\autoref{sec:class-weighting-rule}).

\section{Synthesis and Research Gap}
\label{sec:synthesis-and-gap}
% =============================================================================

This section combines the findings of the preceding sections into one research gap and lists every declared divergence between the implemented system and the reviewed literature.

The reviewed literature establishes several points. The available datasets are small, demographically narrow, recorded in laboratories and inherently imbalanced~\cite{yan2014,davison2018,see2019}, and CASME~II remains the primary single-dataset benchmark under its native leave-one-subject-out protocol (\autoref{sec:corpus}). Motion-based input is preferred by the strongest benchmark systems~\cite{see2019,liong2019a,zhaoS2021}; a strain field adds a further cue~\cite{liong2016}, although on CASME~II only weakly and under a protocol that is not subject-independent; and both the magnification factor and the sequence length have interior optima that can be located only by search~\cite{liX2018,ben2021}. At this data size, capacity added without parameters performs better than capacity added with them~\cite{xia2020b}, and the strongest reviewed CASME~II result is obtained with a transformer-based architecture~\cite{zhang2022}. Accuracy alone is inadequate under class imbalance~\cite{liong2016,see2019} and must be replaced by macro-averaged measures accumulated across folds~\cite{see2019}.

\subsection{Four Limitations Common to the Reviewed Studies}
\label{sec:four-limitations}

The limitations identified in the preceding sections are not independent. Four structural problems account for most of them.

\textbf{(a) Components are isolated one at a time within one proposed design, and never jointly.} Matched comparisons do exist: \autoref{tab:evm-effect} isolates magnification, \autoref{tab:strain-performance} isolates strain against a flow control, Xia et al.~\cite{xia2020b} compare a recurrent convolutional network with three variants that each add one module while all other parameters are held constant, and the comparison of mean and LSTM aggregation in SLSTT isolates its aggregator (\autoref{sec:slstt-results}). No reviewed study, however, varies \textit{several} components together so that their interactions can be observed. Every ablation is internal to one proposed design and changes one factor from that design's defaults, while systems from different papers are compared as wholes, so a difference in reported performance cannot be attributed to any single stage. There is therefore no basis for deciding which learned stages are essential, or whether the value of one component depends on the presence of another.

\textbf{(b) Evaluation protocols are inconsistent and incompletely reported.} Sample counts, label sets and the choice between leave-one-subject-out, leave-one-video-out and leave-one-sample-out evaluation vary between studies~\cite{yan2014,liong2016,qu2016,liX2018}, and the most frequently quoted baseline is described in three different ways by its own sources (\autoref{sec:original-baseline}). Given the fold composition of CASME~II (\autoref{sec:corpus-threats}), these differences change what the reported numbers mean.

\textbf{(c) The strongest results depend on training conditions that a single-dataset study cannot reproduce.} The best CASME~II results come from composite-dataset training~\cite{see2019} (\autoref{sec:megc-metrics}) and, for SLSTT, from ImageNet pre-training~\cite{zhang2022,dosovitskiy2021}, neither of which is a property of the architecture credited with the result. Almost no evidence exists about the behaviour of these components when trained from scratch on one small dataset, which is the situation of most practitioners.

\textbf{(d) Preprocessing parameters have interior optima and are transferred without re-validation.} The magnification factor~\cite{liX2018}, the sequence length~\cite{liX2018,ben2021} and the input resolution~\cite{xia2020b} all show performance that first rises and then falls, and none has a principled selection rule. Values are nevertheless transferred between pipelines with entirely different downstream models: $\alpha = 30$ from a single-frame appearance encoder~\cite{liY2018}, ten-frame interpolation from one baseline paper~\cite{liX2013} to the whole field, and $\lambda = 10^{-4}$ from CIFAR, although its own authors re-tuned it for ImageNet~\cite{yang2021}.

\subsection{The Research Gap}
\label{sec:research-gap}

Limitations (a) to (d) together define the research gap:

\textbf{The reviewed literature has established a standard pipeline for micro-expression recognition~\cite{ben2021,li2022survey,xie2022} (magnify, compute motion, normalise duration, extract spatial features, model time) and contains matched ablations of individual stages, but no study varies several of these stages jointly so that their relative contributions and interactions can be measured. Because components are varied one at a time within a single proposed system, under protocols that differ in unstated ways, and mostly under training conditions richer than a single small dataset provides, the literature cannot show which parts of the pipeline are necessary, which are inherited convention, and which depend on the presence of another.}

This gap has practical consequences. A practitioner assembling this pipeline has no published basis for allocating effort: for judging which stages are the most valuable, whether a component with a negligible average effect may still matter in a particular case, or whether a ranking obtained from an incomplete evaluation on a dataset of this size is stable.

\subsection{How this Thesis Addresses the Gap}
\label{sec:addressing-gap}

\textbf{What it does.} This thesis treats the pipeline as a factorial experiment rather than a proposal. Four components are varied independently across every architecturally valid cell of a 2\textsuperscript{4} design, giving twelve configurations, each evaluated under \textbf{complete 25-fold leave-one-subject-out cross-validation on CASME~II} with all other factors held identical. This provides the joint measurement that limitation (a) identifies as missing; reporting the protocol in full addresses (b) for this study; and training from scratch is the condition that (c) identifies as lacking evidence. The marginal contribution of each component is obtained from pairs of runs that differ in that component alone, and the distribution of the four effects answers the research gap.

\textbf{What it does not do.} Three of the four limitations are addressed only in part. For (b), this thesis reports its own protocol completely but cannot resolve the inconsistency of the field, and its sample size of 156 differs from the 145 of MEGC~\cite{see2019}. For (c), it establishes the behaviour of these components when trained from scratch on one dataset, which is a different question from their behaviour under composite training. For (d), it inherits parameter values rather than searching them: $\alpha = 10$, $T = 32$, $\lambda = 10^{-4}$ and $\gamma = 2.0$ are fixed throughout, so the ablation measures the architecture at a single point of the preprocessing parameter space.

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:ch3-conclusion}
% =============================================================================

This chapter reviewed the published evidence for each component of the pipeline: Eulerian video magnification, optical flow, optical strain, temporal normalisation, the convolutional backbone, parameter-free attention, the temporal transformer and training under class imbalance. For each component, it identified what the reviewed studies establish and where their evidence stops. It then combined these findings into four common limitations and a single research gap: the stages of the standard pipeline have never been varied jointly under one protocol, so their relative contributions are unknown. Finally, it listed the points at which the implemented system departs from the reviewed methods.

Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology} describes the study designed to address this gap: a factorial ablation of four pipeline components on CASME~II, together with the configurations, model architecture, training procedure and evaluation protocol used to carry it out.
